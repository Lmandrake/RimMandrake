# Non-Star-Wars Science Fiction Dungeons — The Derelict

Research domain: sci-fi RPG/videogame "derelict" and ruined-facility dungeons —
maps, obstacle vocabulary, triggers, structure, contents, storytelling-through-space,
and inter-site connections — for RimMaster's Jawa scavenger-clan dungeons.

Status legend: **CONFIRMED** = I read the actual product text, or a source directly
quotes/reproduces it. **UNCERTAIN** = review, wiki summary, or my own inference from
secondary material. Every line below carries its evidence (path or URL). Nothing here
is a fabricated map or room list — where I could not reach primary text I say so and

## 1. Traveller (GDW, 1977–86) — the deckplan tradition

Traveller is CONFIRMED to be real and exactly as valuable as the brief guessed: its
classic adventures are literally keyed deckplans for grid-based ship/station/ruin
maps, structurally close to a RimWorld map. I got one FULL primary text and several
table-of-contents + retrospective-quote sources for the rest.

### 1a. *Twilight's Peak* (Adventure 3, Marc Miller, 1980) — CONFIRMED, full text read

Saved at `sources/scifi_derelict/traveller_A03_twilights_peak.pdf`. This is the single
richest item in this research pass: a genuine multi-level derelict/ruin dungeon with a
deckplan, a room-by-room key, environmental puzzles, and an explicit design/pacing
essay by the author.

**The dungeon has three connected vertical structures**, found in this order:

1. **The Octagon** (a stone tower, 5 levels: Top / Upper / Ground / Lower / Lowest).
   Numbered room key on the deckplan (rooms 1–20 across the 5 levels). Ground level
   has a barred entrance with a skeleton behind it (storytelling-through-space: "any
   other method of entry will show what was holding them closed: a single skeleton
   slumped behind them both"). The building is a **haunted-house pastiche** the
   Referee's Notes explicitly says to "milk... for cheap thrills and scare tactics":
   a lantern on a hidden timer casts moving shadows through the window "to give the
   appearance of someone being on this level," a rusty iron ladder-bar that fails at a
   fixed cumulative-strength threshold (40 points climbed), sleeping bags and crates of
   drugs left by a marooned crew.
2. **The Catacombs** (9 numbered chambers below a hidden trapdoor under the Octagon's
   floor star). Rough-cut natural rock vs. the Octagon's fine masonry — the material
   change alone tells players they've left the "built" structure. Obstacles keyed
   room-by-room: a dry adhesive spiderweb blocking a stairway (str 9+ to pull free),
   small dead animals wrapped in it (foreshadowing), a **trapper** (giant spider)
   guarding its kitten under a stair — attacks fiercely but the design note explicitly
   flags "there is no indication of poison... note that it is cute and cuddly" once its
   motive (protecting young) is revealed. A **bank-vault-style door** that will not
   open by any conventional method (explosives/tools/hacking all explicitly fail) —
   it opens only in response to a **psionic aura of acute fear**, which the trapper's
   attack is designed to supply.
3. **The Alien Base** (a true Ancients installation across the vault door, entered
   either through the door or through side "fissures" that let a party bypass it
   entirely — a genuine branching alternate route). Room key with real obstacle
   variety: automatic sliding doors that won't close on an obstruction; a barracks of
   36 suspended-animation alien warriors in "ovoids" that dissolve and arm in 45
   seconds if triggered; a zero-G sleeping-compartment wing; sanitary-disposal patches
   that are secretly matter-teleporters (touch three times fast to activate, and don't
   stand on one); food dispensers that materialize a hologram-selected meal by
   teleportation (fumble the grab and "the hand will receive a sharp sting"); an
   anti-matter battery ("very high levels of current... potential problem; if all the
   anti-matter is released at once, a large quantity of energy is released" — an
   overload/meltdown hazard object, not a set-piece); a reactor/power room whose
   controls can black out the whole base or activate an escape bridge; a fully
   interactive holographic system-map globe.

**Puzzle-obstacle vocabulary this module alone contributes** (CONFIRMED, verbatim
mechanics): sealed bulkhead/vault door with a non-physical unlock condition (fear,
not force); automatic doors that won't close on a placed object (a bypass exploit,
not a bug); suspended-animation troops that "wake" on tamper/trigger; a
power-plant kill switch that also controls an escape route, coupling two systems so
"turn off the power" has a cost; a light/bridge that **cycles on and off on a timer**
(the cavern bridge glows red/white/dark on a die roll each turn — a moving-platform
hazard that isn't a trap, just an inconvenient physics fact of the base); teleporting
hazards disguised as mundane fixtures (toilet, food slot) — storytelling by *object
function*, not text.

**Structure and pacing — CONFIRMED verbatim from the "Referee's Notes" section**,
which is explicit designer advice on when to spring each beat:
> "This adventure is written to play like a novel or a story. It begins innocently
> enough, and slowly builds to several different climaxes. Each should be carefully
> handled..."
- Act 1, **The Search**: pure investigation/rumor-gathering across a regional map (no
  combat), used to seed lore and set up a red herring (a rival company sabotage plot).
- Act 2, **The Octagon**: horror-pastiche exploration, low lethality, high atmosphere.
- Act 3 trigger, **the Zhodani raid**: "Some time after the group leaves to search for
  Twilight's Peak, the local officials will have second thoughts and send troops after
  them, to silence them permanently... The arrival of these troops should drive the
  adventurers down deeper into the catacombs, the troops right on their heels in a
  running gunbattle." — an explicit **pursuit-forces-descent** trigger: the enemy is a
  pacing tool that pushes the party into the next dungeon layer, not just a fight.
- Act 4, **The Alien Base** payoff: superweapon-grade loot gated behind the fear-door;
  explicit reward list (drugs worth Cr5000/dose, an anti-matter battery "priceless" but
  useless to humans, artifacts).
- **The escape**: Droyne (the base's original owner-descendants) self-destruct the base
  on a timer once their duty (repelling the raid) is done — "Bereft of purpose, they
  will initiate a self-destruct sequence, with a few hours delay to enable the
  adventurers to get clear" — an explicit ticking-clock climax/escape beat, handed to
  the referee as a design instruction, not improvised.

**Also CONFIRMED, reusable-verbatim lore/log-fragment fodder**: "The Epic" — an
in-fiction 100,000-word bad poem written by a marooned scout, which the module has the
players' ship's computer "synopsize" into a clean prose account (reproduced in full,
~600 words) of the wreck, the winter siege by wolves, the first discovery of the
Octagon, and two earlier failed rescue attempts — a worked example of feeding backstory
through an in-world document rather than exposition, directly reusable as RimWorld
inspect/lore text.

### 1b. Other classic Traveller adventures — CONFIRMED structure via ToC + Grognardia
retrospectives (which quote the modules), primary text NOT reached this pass

- ***Death Station*** (Double Adventure 3, Marc Miller, 1981) — CONFIRMED (Grognardia
  retrospective, https://grognardia.blogspot.com/2026/03/retrospective-death-station.html,
  quoting the module): investigate a lab ship where a drug experiment "succeeded but
  sabotage by a rival company resulted in the entire crew being exposed" to an unstable
  combat drug causing physical enhancement plus insanity. Explicitly called "a science
  fictional 'dungeon'" by the reviewer — keyed locations, discovery-or-danger at each.
  Design note: "relies less on direct exposition than on the gradual accumulation of
  clues"; four pages of referee's notes on encounter guidelines. Top-10 ranking (#6)
  cites "insane crewmen, escaped lab animals, and lots of hidden ducts" as its texture.
- ***Research Station Gamma*** (Adventure 2, Marc Miller, 1980) — CONFIRMED ToC via
  wiki.travellerrpg.com (Introduction/Late One Night/Vanejen world/Submersibles/Rhylanor
  Subsector/Rumors/**The Station** (p.17)/**The Globes** (p.26)/**Robots** (p.31)/
  Referee's Notes/Library Data). Setting: an underwater research station reached by
  submersible; opposition includes AN-427-class security robots. Grognardia (Top-10,
  #9): "effectively a 'dungeon crawl in space,'" praised for "unusual architecture."
- ***Leviathan*** (Adventure 4, Bob McWilliams, 1980) — CONFIRMED ToC via wiki + full
  Grognardia retrospective quote (http://grognardia.blogspot.com/2024/12/retrospective-leviathan.html):
  NOT a dungeon-in-a-hull — it's "a collection of aids to the referee," dedicating most
  of its 44 pages to **keyed deckplans and game stats for the ship and its entire
  56-man crew**, plus 26 open-ended rumors — the referee-toolkit model rather than a
  fixed plot. Ranked #2 on Grognardia's Top-10 as "a hexcrawl in space."
- ***Shadows*** (Double Adventure 1, 1980) — CONFIRMED via Grognardia
  (https://grognardia.blogspot.com/2023/04/retrospective-shadows.html): a genuine
  "science fiction dungeon"/"haunted house" — party detects artificial pyramids on a
  planet, is shot down, must explore the pyramid complex, encountering "technological
  hazards, such as inoperable doors and inexplicable machinery," hostile animals, and
  seismic tremors. Top-10 (#7): "pulls off" the dungeon-crawl concept "exceptionally
  well, with lots of interesting details."
- ***Annic Nova*** (paired with Shadows in Double Adventure 1) — CONFIRMED, and
  explicitly a **negative** data point worth recording: per the same Grognardia post,
  it "is little more than the description of an odd starship with an accompanying set
  of deckplans and some unrelated library data" — "to call it a scenario at all is
  being generous." An actual-play thread crossing it with *Alien* (UNCERTAIN — a GM's
  home-brew, not stock content, from https://www.enworld.org/threads/classic-traveller-actual-play-annic-nova-meets-alien.669484/)
  describes rooms as played: a bridge with non-standard alien computers and active
  grav-plates; a workshop leading up a steep corridor to hydroponics (both dead and
  living plants — storytelling through contradiction); two side-corridor gun turrets,
  one "rotated beyond its normal tolerance, so as to target the vessel itself"; a
  quarantined cargo hold; a room "scoured from the inside by laser fire"; a locked room
  holding four alien-larva bodies. Useful as a worked storytelling-through-space
  example even though it is not the module's own text.
- ***Beltstrike*** (Adventure 1 / boxed set; 1982 GDW original, 2008 Mongoose reissue)
  — CONFIRMED, official 6-page sample read in full (saved,
  `traveller_beltstrike_sample_preview.pdf`). This is the **scavenger-fantasy match**
  of the whole Traveller line: not a single dungeon but a linked-scenario belt campaign
  (chapters: *The Factory* → *Signal to Noise* → *Working Them Angels* → *Adventures in
  the Chlaer Radical* → *Belt War*) plus a full **prospecting minigame**: watch-based
  scanning rolls, a Scan Potential Table (crystalline/dense/radioactive/exotic results
  by belt zone), a Composition Table, a Resource Presence Table, and an **Exotic Table**
  whose roll of 12 is "Artefact," 1d6: "1–2 Old starship, probe or satellite debris
  with a traceable origin / 3–4 ...with no traceable origin / 5–6 Old, out-of-system
  technological debris" at escalating tech level — i.e., a real d100-style
  wreck/derelict-encounter random table, directly reusable as a salvage-discovery
  mechanic. Fuller chapter descriptions (CONFIRMED via https://docslib.org/doc/549215/adventure-1-beltstrike):
  *The Factory* = survey then recommission a mining platform for a free company;
  *Working Them Angels* = the recommissioning goes wrong, "betrayal is in the
  characters' midst," ending in "an astonishing discovery"; *Belt War* = corporate
  claim-jump triggers open armed conflict in the belt.
- **Unreached this pass** (named per the contract, not fabricated): full primary text
  of *Death Station*, *Research Station Gamma*, *Leviathan*, *Shadows*, and the full
  *Beltstrike* book beyond its 6-page sample — PDFs exist on `the-eye.eu` and
  `archive.org`'s `rpg.rem.uz/Traveller.tar` mirror (confirmed present via search hits)
  but WebFetch cannot reach `the-eye.eu` at all, and the full Beltstrike/other PDFs
  exceeded the fetch tool's 10 MB content cap. A Fetcher `FILE` job for these was queued

## 2. Mothership (Tuesday Knight Games, 2018–) — CONFIRMED via Wikipedia, primary
module text unreached

Sci-fi horror OSR-style game. CONFIRMED (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothership_(role-playing_game)):
percentile-under-attribute system; **Stress and Panic** are explicit mechanical
interrupts to the action economy — panic checks trigger at stress thresholds and can
knock a character out of acting, which is a directly transferable "creeping dread"
mechanic. Four classes (Teamster, Scientist, Android, Marine). Core modules: *Dead
Planet* (2018, won the 2019 ENNIE for Best Adventure), *A Pound of Flesh* (2019),
*Gradient Descent* (2020, bundled with the Deluxe boxed set alongside the other three
— i.e. official Deluxe-set canon groups these four together), plus the *Warden's
Operations Manual* (2024) which introduces a "T.O.M.B.S. cycle" for procedural
scenario generation (name UNCERTAIN in detail — I could not reach the manual's text to
confirm what T.O.M.B.S. stands for or how the cycle works).
**Unreached**: room keys/deckplans for *Dead Planet*, *Gradient Descent*, *Another Bug
Hunt*, *A Pound of Flesh*, *Ypsilon 14* — every targeted search for these hit
Mothership-the-trucking-company/Mothership.sg instead of the RPG (a search-engine
collision I could not route around with the tools available this session), and
RPGGeek/DriveThruRPG blocked WebFetch with 403s. Given the owner's stated priority on
*Gradient Descent* as an "exhaustive extraction" target, this is the single biggest
gap in this pass and the best next target.

## 3. Alien: The Roleplaying Game (Free League, 2019–) — CONFIRMED system, scenario
content UNREACHED

CONFIRMED via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien:_The_Roleplaying_Game: Year Zero Engine
(6=success on d6 pools; harder actions = fewer dice, not higher targets); **Stress and
Panic dice** mechanically shift outcomes (same design lineage as Mothership). Two
explicit modes: **Campaign** (GM-built, open) vs. **Cinematic** (a pre-written scenario
with 3–5 pre-gen sessions meant to run in one sitting) — a clean, citable structural
split for "how long is this experience" metrics. The 2019 core box ships with a
cinematic scenario, *Chariots of the Gods*. Design philosophy, quoted: players "are
not at the top of the food chain... in every sense, the prey" — survival horror over
combat dominance; environmental hazards (radiation, disease, starvation) rated as
dangerous as the xenomorph. **Named but unreached**: *Destroyer of Worlds*,
*Heart of Darkness*, *Building Better Worlds* — Free League's own product pages
404'd, DriveThruRPG 403'd WebFetch, and RPGGeek 403'd WebFetch; Fetcher searches for
these collided with the *Alien* film's Wikipedia/IMDb pages rather than the RPG
products. No room lists or deckplans obtained for any Alien RPG scenario this pass —
treat all specific-scenario detail as unreached, not summarized-from-title.

## 4. Metamorphosis Alpha (TSR, 1976) — CONFIRMED, first sci-fi RPG megadungeon

CONFIRMED via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphosis_Alpha: generation starship *Warden*
suffered catastrophic failure; survivors no longer understand it and treat it as their
whole world/universe. Explicitly dungeon-structured: players explore "hex by hex in
the 'wilderness' or square by square in the dungeons of the engineering sectors" — a
**17-level ship** literally repurposed as a D&D-style dungeon (mechanics directly
borrowed from D&D, per the article). Predates *Traveller* by a year; contests with Ken
St. Andre's *Starfaring* (also 1976) for "first sci-fi RPG." This is the origin point
for "the whole ship IS the dungeon" as a genre move, worth citing as the ancestor of
every derelict/megadungeon on this list.

## 5. Star Frontiers (TSR, 1982–85) — CONFIRMED via Wikipedia

CONFIRMED (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Frontiers): the launch trilogy is one connected
world-as-dungeon arc — **SF-0 *Crash on Volturnus*** (party stranded), **SF-1
*Volturnus, Planet of Mystery***, **SF-2 *Starspawn of Volturnus***. Noted by RPG
historian Stu Horvath as unusual: a space-opera game that shipped two years *without
spaceship rules*, i.e., the opening content was deliberately planet/ruin-bound, not
ship-bound. No room-by-room detail reached this pass (unreached).

## 7. Numenera (Monte Cook Games, 2013–) — CONFIRMED, strong scavenger-fantasy fit

CONFIRMED via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numenera: set ~1 billion years future-Earth, "the
Ninth World" (eight prior civilizations fallen); "numenera" = the salvageable
tech-artifacts themselves, the game's core resource. Native tech level is medieval —
advanced ruins read as magic (Clarke's Third Law played straight). Named supplements:
*Jade Colossus: Ruins of the Prior Worlds* (a campaign built around one "gigantic
ruin," with a "Numenera Ruin Mapping Engine" — a generation tool, unreached in detail);
*Vertices* ("eight adventures in prior-world ruins"); *Slaves of the Machine God*. No
room keys reached — but the core premise (salvage from a fallen precursor civilization
as the central resource loop) is close enough to the Jawa fantasy to be worth a full
follow-up pass specifically on *Jade Colossus*.

## 8. Environmental storytelling in sci-fi video games — CONFIRMED, strong worked
examples

- **System Shock (1994)** — CONFIRMED via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_Shock: no NPCs
  to talk to; the entire plot is delivered via "log discs and e-mails" left in the
  environment. Designer Austin Grossman named the explicit literary model: Edgar Lee
  Masters' *Spoon River Anthology* — "a series of short speeches from people, that when
  put together, gave you a history of a place." This is the cleanest possible citation
  for "storytelling through space/objects, no dialogue" as a deliberate, named
  technique, not just an emergent effect.
- **Dead Space (2008)** — CONFIRMED via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Space_(2008_video_game):
  narrative was defined FIRST, then levels/objectives built around it (story-first
  level design, not level-first). Deliberately avoided sterile sci-fi in favor of
  Gothic-influenced industrial spaces; lighting modeled on "strong lamps used in
  dentistry" for clinical unease. Explicit design point: ordinary, recognizable rooms
  (corridors, medical bays) were made unsettling by familiarity, not by exoticism —
  directly applicable to a RimWorld interior.
- **Prey (2017)** — CONFIRMED via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prey_(2017_video_game):
  designer Ricardo Bare explicitly called Talos I a **"mega-dungeon" adapted for
  space**, "a skyscraper floating in space" — open/interconnected rather than
  mission-partitioned, with multiple entries to each area and content deliberately
  gated behind player-ability unlocks (a Metroidvania-style dungeon-return loop).
  Environmental storytelling delivered through "books, notes, and audio logs" that
  players can freely ignore — Bare: giving players the choice "to examine or
  completely ignore them" is what made the station "feel more 'real.'" Architectural
  diversity (1960s retrofuturism → Soviet brutalism → Art Deco) used to encode which
  era/faction built which wing, without a word of dialogue.
- **Duskers** — CONFIRMED via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duskers: mechanically the closest
  video-game analog to indirect RimWorld-style control named in the brief. Two-layer
  structure: metagame (fleet/resource management between derelicts) and boarding
  (command-line ordering of up to 4 drones per derelict, e.g. `Nav 1 R2`). Hazards =
  radiation leaks plus hostile life/robots; core tactic explicitly named is **luring
  enemies into other rooms** rather than fighting them head-on. Currency is scrap;
  disabled drones/upgrades must be *towed back through the airlock* to bank a gain —
  a real risk/reward extraction mechanic (you can lose a derelict's rewards by dying
  before hauling them out), worth stealing directly for RimWorld salvage runs.

## 9. Space Hulk (Games Workshop board game, 1989) — CONFIRMED, board-only, real
tile system

CONFIRMED via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Hulk: modular board tiles representing
corridors/rooms "lock together like a jigsaw puzzle," letting missions recombine the
same tile set into new layouts — a direct, physical precedent for procedurally
recombining a fixed room-tile library, which is exactly the kind of "author once,
recombine per-site" approach a RimWorld dungeon-generation pass could use. Asymmetric
design: slow ranged Terminators vs. fast melee Genestealers, hidden-information tension
(concealed command points vs. concealed enemy count) baked into a derelict-boarding
premise. (Note: Warhammer 40k's *Rogue Trader* RPG, checked separately, does NOT
foreground space-hulk exploration — that's this board game's territory, not the RPG's.)
