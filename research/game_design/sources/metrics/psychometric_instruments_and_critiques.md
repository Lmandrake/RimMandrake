## GEQ — Game Experience Questionnaire

**Origin:** Poels, K., de Kort, Y., & IJsselsteijn, W. (2007/2013). "D3.3: Game
Experience Questionnaire" — deliverable of the EU FUGA (Fun of Gaming) project,
Eindhoven University of Technology. **Never formally peer-reviewed or published as
a journal article** — it exists as an unpublished technical report/manual. This is
itself the root of the critique below. (Existence + authorship + status confirmed
via OpenAlex record and via the critique paper's own abstract, next entry.)

**The critique (CONFIRMED, full abstract read verbatim):** Law, E. L.-C.,
Brühlmann, F., & Mekler, E. D. (2018). "Systematic Review and Validation of the
Game Experience Questionnaire (GEQ) — Implications for Citation and Reporting
Practice." Proc. CHI PLAY 2018. DOI: 10.1145/3242671.3242683. 199 citations
(at time of lookup).
- Systematically reviewed 73 publications that used the GEQ.
- Found the questionnaire "operationalized in 24 distinct ways" across the 42 of
  those studies that reported enough detail to classify.
- Ran their own validation study, **N = 633**: found **"no evidence for [the]
  originally postulated 7-factor structure of GEQ."**
- Verbatim risk framing: inconsistent reporting + uncited/miscited item counts risk
  "proliferation of erroneous study implications" across the field.
- **This is the single most important "a discredited instrument confidently applied
  is worse than no instrument" finding for this brief.** GEQ is ubiquitous in games
  research and its core factor structure has been directly tested and not
  supported, by a study nearly 5x the size of many original validations in the
  field.

## PENS — Player Experience of Need Satisfaction

**Origin:** Ryan, R. M., Rigby, C. S., & Przybylski, A. K. (2006). "Player
Experience of Need Satisfaction Scale." PsycTESTS Dataset. DOI: 10.1037/t71188-000.
(Confirmed via OpenAlex; the instrument entry itself, not a narrative paper.)

**Theoretical grounding (CONFIRMED, full abstract read verbatim):** Przybylski,
A. K., Rigby, C. S., & Ryan, R. M. (2010). "A Motivational Model of Video Game
Engagement." Review of General Psychology, 14(2), 154–166.
DOI: 10.1037/a0019440. Grounds PENS in Self-Determination Theory: competence,
autonomy, relatedness as the three basic psychological needs games can satisfy;
reviews evidence linking need satisfaction to short-term well-being, and also
covers violent-content effects, aggression, immersion determinants, and
disordered/problematic engagement.

**Joint validation study (CONFIRMED, existence + methodology; full text not read —
only search-engine paraphrase, which gave inconsistent framing across two runs, so
treat conclusions here as UNCERTAIN pending a direct read):** Johnson, D., Gardner,
M. J., & Perry, R. (2018). "Validation of two game experience scales: The Player
Experience of Need Satisfaction (PENS) and Game Experience Questionnaire (GEQ)."
International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 118, 38–46.
DOI: 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2018.05.003. Ran EFA (N=629) then CFA (N=729). One AI-mediated
summary of this paper claimed it supports both scales' validity; another summary of
the same DOI (in the context of the GEQ critique search) implied nuance. **Do not
cite this paper's conclusion as settled without reading the actual text** — flagging
explicitly per the no-fabrication rule.

## PXI — Player Experience Inventory

**Origin (preliminary):** Vanden Abeele, V., Nacke, L. E., Mekler, E. D., &
Johnson, D. (2016). "Design and preliminary validation of The Player Experience
Inventory." Proc. CHI PLAY Companion 2016. DOI: 10.1145/2968120.2987744.

**Full validation (CONFIRMED, full abstract read verbatim):** Vanden Abeele, V.,
Spiel, K., Nacke, L. E., Johnson, D., & Gerling, K. (2019). "Development and
validation of the player experience inventory: A scale to measure player
experiences at the level of functional and psychosocial consequences."
International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 135, 102370.
DOI: 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2019.102370.
- Grounded in Means-End Theory: **Functional Consequences** (immediate experience
  from a design choice — audiovisual appeal, ease-of-control) vs. **Psychosocial
  Consequences** (second-order emotional experience — immersion, mastery).
  This functional/psychosocial split is directly useful for separating "is this
  dungeon comfortable to operate" from "did it feel meaningful."
- Item/construct development: 2 iterations with 64 Games User Research experts.
- Validated across 5 studies, 529 participants total; supports discriminant and
  convergent validity and configural invariance across samples.
- German translation/validation: Graf et al. (2022), Mensch und Computer 2022,
  DOI: 10.1145/3543758.3543763 — N=506, confirmed the 10-factor model but flagged
  room to improve discriminant validity (so even PXI is not beyond critique).

**Abbreviated versions (two DIFFERENT scales — do not conflate):**
- miniPXI: Haider, Harteveld, Johnson, Birk, Mandryk, Seif El-Nasr, Nacke, Gerling
  & Vanden Abeele (2022). Proc. ACM HCI, Vol. 6 (CHI PLAY).
  DOI: 10.1145/3549507. 11 items. Per-construct single-item reliability estimates
  ranged 0.51–0.83, average ≈0.68 across PXI constructs — a real, sourced number
  for "how much you lose by shortening the instrument."
- mPXI ("Mini Player Experience Inventory," a separate, earlier abbreviation
  effort): Harteveld, Javvaji, Machado, Zastavker, Bennett & Abdoun (2020).
  Extended Abstracts, CHI PLAY 2020. DOI: 10.1145/3383668.3419877.

## CEGEQ — Core Elements of Gaming Experience Questionnaire

**Status: NOT independently confirmed this session.** Attributed in the literature
to Calvillo-Gámez, Cairns & Cox (commonly cited as their "core elements of the
gaming experience"/puppetry model work, ~2009–2010), but repeated targeted searches
this session (OpenAlex title search, author-combination search) returned zero
matches for the originating paper. **Confirmed only indirectly**, via a paper that
*uses* the named instrument: Chesham, A., Wyss, P., Müri, R. M., Mosimann, U. P., &
Nef, T. (2017). "Genre Preferences and Acceptance of Casual Games in Older Adults."
JMIR Serious Games. DOI: 10.2196/games.7025 — 16 older adults (65+) rated 7 casual
games across 4 genres using CEGEQ (enjoyment, gameplay understanding, control,
ownership dimensions); puzzle games scored highest on enjoyment (0.95/1.00 scale).
Treat CEGEQ's existence as real (it's cited as an operational instrument by a
peer-reviewed paper) but its authorship/item-count/validation status as UNVERIFIED
until the origin paper is located and read directly.

## GUESS — Game User Experience Satisfaction Scale

Phan, M. H., Keebler, J. R., & Chaparro, B. S. (2016). "The Development and
Validation of the Game User Experience Satisfaction Scale (GUESS)." Human Factors,
The Journal of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society.
DOI: 10.1177/0018720816669646. Nine subscales; tested across >450 unique game
titles; reported content and discriminant validity. (Abstract paraphrase only.)

## CORGIS — Challenge Originating from Recent Gameplay Interaction Scale

Denisova, A., Cairns, P., Guckelsberger, C., & Zendle, D. (2019). "Measuring
perceived challenge in digital games: Development & validation of the challenge
originating from recent gameplay interaction scale (CORGIS)." International
Journal of Human-Computer Studies. DOI: 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2019.102383. A validated
instrument specifically for PERCEIVED CHALLENGE (not the broader GEQ bundle) —
directly useful as the "challenge/difficulty" leg of a dungeon rubric if a human
playtest is run. Companion: Denisova, A., & Cairns, P. (2018). "Player experience
and deceptive expectations of difficulty adaptation in digital games."
Entertainment Computing. DOI: 10.1016/j.entcom.2018.12.001 — players' difficulty
perception can be manipulated/expectation-biased independent of actual difficulty.

## Immersion — Jennett et al.'s three-level model + questionnaire

Jennett, C., Cox, A. L., Cairns, P., Dhoparee, S., Epps, A., Tijs, T., & Walton,
A. M. (2008). "Measuring and defining the experience of immersion in games."
International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 66(9), 641–661.
DOI: 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2008.04.004. **2,005 citations** — one of the most-cited
papers in this entire research area. Existence, full author list, exact venue/
volume/pages CONFIRMED via OpenAlex. **The specific three-level model content
(engagement/engrossment/total immersion) and exact item count are from general
domain knowledge, NOT independently re-verified from the primary text this
session** — the abstract text itself was unavailable through OpenAlex and a guessed
open-access PDF URL 404'd. Flag as UNCERTAIN until the actual paper is read.

## Physiological / affective measurement (non-questionnaire)

Nacke, L. E., & Lindley, C. A. (2010). "Affective Ludology, Flow and Immersion in
a First-Person Shooter: Measurement of Player Experience." arXiv:1004.0248. (Full
abstract read verbatim.) Combines subjective questionnaires with objective EMG
(electromyography) and EDA (electrodermal activity) across 3 Half-Life 2 level-
design mods. A combat-oriented level produced significantly higher high-arousal
positive-affect readings — demonstrating level design measurably shifts
psychophysiological state, independent of what players later self-report.

Jensen, M. F., Dixen, L., & Burelli, P. (2024). "Hades Again and Again: A Study on
Frustration Tolerance, Physiology and Player Experience." arXiv:2401.14878. Cross-
references questionnaires, gameplay data, and heart rate in the roguelike Hades to
study individual differences in frustration tolerance. (Abstract read; the paper's
actual numeric findings were not visible in the fetched excerpt — flag for
follow-up if frustration/tedium metrics become a priority.)

## IMI — Intrinsic Motivation Inventory (as used in games)

**Not independently confirmed this session.** Repeated targeted OpenAlex queries
surfaced only tangential IMI applications (a Multidimensional Work Motivation Scale
validation, a schizophrenia-research adaptation, a neuroscience-of-motivation
review) — none is the foundational psychometric paper (commonly attributed in
secondary sources to McAuley, Duncan & Tammen 1989, and to Ryan's earlier 1982
work) nor a games-specific application. Treat IMI's use in games research as real
but UNSOURCED this session; do not cite an item count or subscale list for it.

## Adjacent/related instruments noted but not central

- GAMEFULQUEST (Högberg, Hamari & Wästlund, 2019, User Modeling and User-Adapted
  Interaction, DOI 10.1007/s11257-019-09223-w) — measures perceived *gamefulness*
  of a gamified (non-game) service, not core player experience of a game.
- Game Immersion Questionnaire (Cheng, She & Annetta, 2014, Journal of Computer
  Assisted Learning, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12066) — a DIFFERENT immersion instrument
  from Jennett et al., built for game-based science learning; hierarchical factor
  structure, "good reliability and validity" per abstract paraphrase.
- ITC-SOPI (Lessiter, Freeman, Keogh & Davidoff, 2001, PRESENCE, DOI
  10.1162/105474601300343612) — a cross-media PRESENCE questionnaire, not
  game-specific; 1,216 citations; relevant only as a methodological ancestor.
