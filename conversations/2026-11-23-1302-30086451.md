The core of the idea is good, Mandrake, and I would change the task you give the weak reader. I launched a pilot of the changed version: `RIMMANDRAKE_TRIAL_WEAK_READER_ACTS_ON_OLD_AND_NEW_RULE_TEXT`.

**The insight worth keeping:** a weaker reader shows a clarity difference that a strong reader hides. Opus reads a muddled rule correctly anyway, so it scores near the top on both texts and the comparison shows nothing. That part is clever and, as far as I know, new here.

**Where the idea as stated breaks:** asking Sonnet "do these two texts mean the same?" cannot tell you which text it misread. A "they differ" verdict could come from the old text being confusing, from the new text being confusing, or from Sonnet being a poor comparer. It also measures comparing, when what you care about is acting.

**The changed design**
- **Sonnet acts on one text.** A fresh Sonnet reader gets one rule text, old or new, and one real situation, and says what an agent must do. A different fresh reader gets the other text and the same situation. A reader never sees both.
- **A key decides.** It is written from the source of truth (the code, your dated words) and not from either wording. A grader that does not know which text an answer came from scores it.
- **Four outcomes stay apart:** correct act, wrong act, "the text does not tell me", and a failed call.
- **Three kinds of change are scored separately,** because the rewrite did all three:
  - style: shorter, active sentences;
  - binding: a term defined where it stands;
  - correction: a false or stale line fixed.
- **A control:** an unchanged section must score the same in both arms.
- **Your premise is checked too:** the same cases go to Opus readers, to see whether Opus really does score near the top on both.
- **The prediction is committed before the first reader runs:** what result means it helps, what means it does not, and what means the instrument cannot see.
