Can an agent sit in the player's chair?

Three designs for a tester of what the game feels like, reviewed by a second AI and rechecked against the files and papers. 31 October 2026.

Is this even feasible? Partly. An agent can be a scout who finds things a player will run into and brings the evidence. It cannot, today, be a judge of fun. Nobody we found has tested an agent against real players' feelings in a long colony game.

How would an agent decide? It would not ask how it feels. Each feeling gets turned into something that can be checked, as in the table below.

What I recommend. Build the scout. Start with a cheap recorder, then run short tests from saved moments, then put the reports in front of you beside a plain screenshot review. If it does not beat the plain review for your time, stop. Dull stays a list of stretches for a person to rank, and I would still ask for one real outside player.

The three designs, side by side

Each report below is about the same real moment: a cook bill sits active on a stove, the raw food is outside the bill's ingredient radius, and nobody cooks.

The reports are invented to show the format. No run has happened. Whether the game explains itself in this moment is exactly what a run would find out.

The Stranger

An agent that plays cold, seeing only what a player sees.

How it works

A fresh agent plays a copy of your save with only the screen, the mouse, and tooltips. A wall stops it reading the game's insides. A separate grader sees the truth and marks its predictions.

Example report

Confusing, 2 of 3 fresh runs. Goal: get these people fed. I expected the cook to start within the hour because the bill reads active. A game day passed. My clicks were confirmed and no cooking job started. I opened the bill and hovered it, and found no reason. I gave up on the goal. This agent got stuck; whether people do is unconfirmed.

Best at

Surprises nobody planned for. First contact.

Worst at

Telling bad design from a clumsy agent. Runs share blind spots, and it can stay stuck in the first hour.

Rough cost

5 to 8 days to build. About 2 to 3 hours and 9 million tokens a run, plus a half-hour game load.

The Flight Recorder

No opinions. It measures the game while anyone plays.

How it works

A small mod logs each order from click to result and whether the screen showed anything. A sweep checks text on the real rendered screen. Numbers are set beside the same numbers from vanilla.

Example report

Stalled order. Stove bill active for 1.3 game days, 0 jobs started, 0 reasons shown on screen. Same pattern on vanilla stove bills: 1 in 40 sessions. Not a judgement: a place to look.

Best at

Cheap, repeatable, catches things getting worse between builds. Pays off whatever else we do.

Worst at

Blind to confusion unless a person is playing. Chasing its numbers could produce a nagging game.

Rough cost

3 to 4 days for the useful part. Almost no tokens. Needs someone or something playing.

The Intent Reviewer

A critic that knows the design and your past rulings.

How it works

It watches film of the game beside what the design meant to happen, and says where the screen fails to show it. Your taste, the screen's clarity and outside players' views stay in separate boxes.

Example report

Communication gap. The design wants food to be a problem the player can solve. These frames show the problem and no visible cause. Resembles your ruling on machines that look connected and are not. Likely you care. No evidence from strangers. Facts checked against the game.

Best at

Uses no game time. Catches a design idea not reaching the screen.

Worst at

It can only echo you, which is the opposite of an empty chair. It may have under 20 real examples to learn from, and it sees only film someone else shot.

Rough cost

4 to 5 days to build, 2 to 5 million tokens a batch, and your labelling time, which is the real cost.

A fourth design appeared: the Checkpoint Auditor

The second AI reviewer read my three and chose none of them.

It starts from a saved moment with one player goal and a short budget, say the minute before that cook bill. Fresh agents each try it. Every step has a prediction, proof the click landed, and a bundle of evidence. A person can make the same prediction from the same screenshot, which tests directly whether the agent stumbles where people do.

Best at: reaching midgame without paying for the opening hour, and repeatable findings.

Worst at: a staged moment is not one reached in play, and it only looks where someone thought to look. I would add one roaming run to keep some surprise.

Rough cost: 6 to 9 days. The trial is about 25 million tokens and 6 to 11 hours of game time.

And the plain benchmark: a real person, watched. It is the only direct evidence of boredom, and it needs no building. It is not perfect: two hours may never reach the late chores, and one person is one opinion. Every design above should have to beat it, or a plain screenshot review.

How we would test it against your judgment

You would not have to trust any of this, and none of it needs you to play.

  1. Your own past complaints. Rebuild a handful of things you really noticed. Does it find them unprompted?
  2. A blind sort, about an hour. 20 to 30 reports, shuffled, some written by a plain cheap method you cannot tell apart. For each: true? new to you? do you care? would a stranger hit it? "I already knew" counts as a hit.
  3. Planted faults. Another agent plants faults in a throwaway copy. This proves it can notice, not that the faults matter.
  4. Which was duller? Ten pairs of stretches. For interest only: 7 of 10 happens by luck 17% of the time.
  5. Worth your time. Minutes of your attention per report you keep. If that loses to a plain screenshot review, we stop.
The limit. You know the design, so you cannot be a stranger. These tests measure agreement with you, not with players. Something you reject may still be real for a newcomer, and only a real newcomer settles that.

What the review changed

  • I was wrong that the agent cannot hover. The bridge has a hover tool. Whether a tooltip draws readably in a screenshot is untested, and it is now the first job.
  • I overstated dull. I said it was impossible because agents do not get bored. It need not feel boredom to predict yours. What is missing is evidence, not a law.
  • Several papers were misquoted. I reopened eight. Three no longer support the claims they were cited for, and the rest needed corrected numbers. No paper was invented.
  • Two rules were thrown out. "Two of three runs must agree" keeps shared mistakes and loses rare ones. Comparing with vanilla helps calibrate, but cannot veto a finding.
  • Corrected facts: the game load is 23 to 30 minutes, and the bridge has 246 tools, not 141.

Where the reviewers still disagree

  • What to build. I first chose recorder, then Stranger, with the reviewer as last filter. The second AI chose the Checkpoint Auditor. I now side with it on the shape and keep a roaming run, because I value surprise more than it does.
  • Whether real players are required. The second AI says yes, at the centre. I treat a person as a separate ask, since you are not playing now. This one is yours.
  • Whether the reviewer may filter. I wanted it to drop reports on taste. The second AI says that removes the very surprises you want. Now it may remove false and duplicate reports, and only sort the rest.

Order of work I recommend

  1. Hover check, 15 minutes when the bridge is free. If tooltips cannot be read, the design shrinks to recorder plus a person.
  2. The cheap half of the recorder. Useful whichever way the rest goes.
  3. One trial: six saved moments, three fresh runs each, one roaming run, planted faults, then your blind sort.
  4. Use the reviewer as a clerk only, for duplicates and tidying.
  5. Ask for one outside player in parallel.

The trial waits on an open watchdog repair. Costs throughout are estimates. Only the game load time is measured.

What I need from you

  2. Dull: are you content with a ranked list and never a verdict?
  3. The four words: does the table say what you mean, dull especially?
  4. A real person: may one outside tester see the private mod list? Or would you give 30 minutes a week with the recorder on?
  5. Your hour: will you do the blind sort and ten pairs? Without it nothing is validated.
  6. The spend: about 25 million tokens and 6 to 11 game hours, on which machine, and how shared with other testing?
  7. Player model: the strongest model throughout, or a cheaper one playing, which is unproven?
  8. Steam reviews: drop mining them unless you pick the reviewer path? I recommend dropping.
