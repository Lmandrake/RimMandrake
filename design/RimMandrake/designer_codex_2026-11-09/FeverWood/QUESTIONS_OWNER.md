# FeverWood

Your chosen experience:

> A world in the treetops above a black mirror nobody touches. You live on the branches, and the water remembers.

Everything below is my proposal, including the written scenes and guesses.

**Proposed root:** FeverWood is a home on living branches above black water that keeps the consequences of your choices. Rooms, paths, growing ground and nectar herds make life above worth protecting; heavy buildings in the marsh need stilts. Below, the pools open onto one vast creature whose limbs threaten, watch and offer treasure. Returning its young earns a later gift; an escaped young can settle in the water and grow into a new danger, even after an earlier victory. Fighting can buy breathing room or lasting safety until that danger returns. These consequences should outlast the outing that caused them. Living bait can bring enemies into an uncertain, staggered clash. A hive expedition risks an alarm and a blocked retreat. Hot weather doubles the oil harvest but fills the lower ground with haze a spark can ignite. Ordinary encounters reveal the deep through its limbs; its whole body's rising belongs to the separate campaign story.

The risk in this proposal: the encounters could be interesting while everyday life still feels like an ordinary swamp settlement.

### 1. What makes the branches feel like home?

**Written picture:** A colonist leaves a room hollowed into living wood, passes the nectar herd and follows a branch path to work. Below, something valuable has been placed beside a pool. The colony could leave it, send a pair down or prepare a safer shore visit.

**Question:** What daily decision is missing from that scene that would make living above the water feel like your FeverWood?

**Why this matters:** The home needs to shape ordinary decisions as strongly as the encounters below.

**My guess:** Work and herd income anchor life above; repeated temptations below interrupt that routine.

### 2. How far away can help be?

**Written picture:** A worker gathering clay is taken at the shore. A companion is nearby on the causeway; another colonist is back at the settlement. Someone must reach the victim and carry them out.

**Question:** From where should a rescuer still have a fair chance: the nearby work party, the settlement or another distance?

**Why this matters:** Rescue timing determines whether shore work calls for a companion or allows help to arrive from home.

**My guess:** A nearby companion should usually have a chance; relying on someone across the settlement should be a poor plan.

### 3. How long before the young becomes a greater danger?

**Written picture:** An escaped young reaches a pool and settles there. It starts small and will mature. The colony knows where it went and must decide how urgently to respond.

**Question:** How much time should pass before it matures, and what should the colony still be able to do during that interval?

**Why this matters:** Growth needs a meaningful period for action; its pace and possible intervention remain unresolved.

**My guess:** Several days would allow a deliberate response, with a chance to remove the young before it matures.

**Recommended demonstration order:** A working treetop home with a shore rescue, then the young's return or escape, the baited clash and the hive expedition.
