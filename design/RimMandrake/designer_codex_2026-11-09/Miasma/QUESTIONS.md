PROPOSED ROOT

Miasma is a stinking, beautiful nursery at the meeting of river and dying salt sea. Its crowded channels and rainbow growth offer hope in a place that is still dangerous: irregular surges move the salt line, leave young behind, and replenish the salvage caught in the roots. The colony must live with that changing shore, read what the brine has reached, and choose where to put its labour. A water-bound warden mother can hear young she cannot reach; people can return them and earn her tolerance, or sell them for a fortune and lose that relationship for good. Young that choose the colony can do useful work in the water and may inherit a smaller, weaker watch when she dies; her age must be apparent before that loss. Rot becomes electricity in decay cells, whose spent beds take corpses down to bones and named skulls and, with Cuisine, grow food. Silt and salt become attar for art and old scars. Ordinary disease remains dangerous, but surviving it here can leave a lasting change. The scuttlers, composters, predators and harmless blooms make this a living, crowded delta rather than a sequence of unrelated rewards. These connected promises need separate proofs and a demonstration of how they meet in an ordinary colony.

My claim: the moving shore makes the colony responsible for lives and useful things it could otherwise treat as waste.
The strongest objection: the paragraph can sound as though one continuous playable loop has already been proved. It has not. Some links are partial or missing, including the surge making new orphan pools, real guarding by the successor and the promised rot feed. This is a proposed player promise, not a passing-test report.

This is a bank of eight drafts, not eight questions to answer in one sitting. Start with 01–02; after independent review, serve at most three at a time and let the answers prune the rest. A phrase, a different scene or “this doesn't matter to me” is enough. No reply is assumed.

Miasma already bundles the nursery, power and corpse beds, attar, salvage, and living ecology. These questions clarify their behaviour and demonstrations; none offers to discard a built capability.

A surgeon is tending fever, haulers are working, and one rescue route crosses a dangerous stretch of roots. The mother cannot do the land crossing for you.

Question: What would a good rescue effort look like that day, and what loss should still be possible after you make that effort?

Why it matters: F2/F3. The rescue and the possibility of failure are already in scope. Their demands on an ordinary colony are not fixed by a random spawn count or a drying timer.

My guessed answer — a guess, not yours: A real expedition with a few people, rather than every colonist stopping to carry babies; a difficult or delayed rescue can fail, but a prompt prepared party has a useful chance.

What changes in the tree: If a few people should cope, tune pool lifetimes, travel and spawn distribution around that scene and prove it with normal jobs. If it should become a colony emergency, require a visible reason to mobilise and a test of that larger commitment. If triage is the point, require enough information to choose which pool to reach first. No answer invents guaranteed survival or removes the built rescue.

05. What rot power earns in an ordinary base

Picture to react to (written thought experiment, not a capture or staged game): The colony has a steady waste stream, a few decay cells, batteries and other generators. One cell is running low and another is near becoming a rotting bed. Today's raw-food filter does not deliver the already-promised rot feed; that is a code gap, not the choice here.

Question: What job should those cells do in the base's power supply, and what daily work should earning that power take?

Why it matters: F2/F3. A generator can pass an output-number test while it is too feeble to matter or so useful that the delta becomes a fuel chore. The role in colony life sets the tuning.

My guessed answer — a guess, not yours: A useful local contribution from things the colony was throwing away, with hauling and eventual replacement as real costs; enough to plan around without making every other generator pointless.

What changes in the tree: If it is steady supply, require useful ordinary loads over several days with actual feeding and replacement. If it is reserve power, require a meaningful outage scene and an honest way to see the reserve. If it mainly supports a small shoreline workshop, use that scale for the acceptance load. These answers tune output, consumption and lifetime; rot feeding and spent-bed conversion are already owed.

06. Why go back into the roots

Farther along the channel are metal, a brined wreck and a crying young. Travelling there costs a working afternoon and exposure to the delta's predators.

Question: What would make the longer salvage trip worth planning, and what should the crew be able to judge before setting out?

Why it matters: F2/F3. Loose goods, wrecks and rescue already exist. Without a useful difference between trips, restocking can become free income or busywork. This asks about the existing salvage's value and information.

My guessed answer — a guess, not yours: The short trip keeps the colony supplied; a larger visible find justifies a prepared outing. I would let the crew see enough to estimate the work and danger, without revealing every wreck reward.

What changes in the tree: If salvage is ordinary supply, require recurring useful quantities and reasonable hauling in the routine-colony scene. If the important payoff is occasional wreck recovery, require a recognisable find and an ordinary salvage job with risk and reward. If exploration is meant to reveal the value, make discovery itself readable and test the return trip. Add no auction, appraisal minigame or new quest from the old unchosen pitches.
