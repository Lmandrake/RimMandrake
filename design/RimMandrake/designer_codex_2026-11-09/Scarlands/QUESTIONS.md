DRY RUN — Scarlands / Warscar, stages 0 and 1. The proposed root, pictures, questions and guesses are agent text. Nothing here is an owner answer, approval or sent question.

PROPOSED ROOT

Warscar makes scavenging an old battlefield feel cursed for reasons the player can see and act on. Its poisoned ground supports creatures that live on the war's remains; apparently placid armour can turn murderous, a breathing stretch of wall can stand up, and a hunter can be known by the traces around its invisible body. When the wind stills, toxic dust falls and records movement; returning wind lifts that record away. Broken guns follow passers-by without firing. The same ruins offer powerful prizes: working projectors to carry home, chemistry to harvest, manuals that unlock practical improvements, and deserted machines whose names and histories survive repair or failure. Staying leaves a lasting mark that teaches useful work as it burdens the survivor. Protection and knowledge make expeditions more capable, and the technology remains broadly useful beyond this biome. The free biome holds that experience on its own; campaign discoveries and ship-vermin behaviour belong to their existing neighbours. Each built promise needs a demonstration, including where the promises meet.

Basis: the owner's selected experience, recorded in `design/RimMandrake/biome_experiences_2026-10-20.md:32`: “Poisoned forever, mad beasts, silent guns. Everyone calls it cursed, and everyone who scavenges here learns why.” That sentence was selected by him; the paragraph above is my interpretation of it. The source file's parenthetical “not yet in Baroque Biomes” is historical: the compose manifest now includes Scarlands as Warscar.

My claim: the curse becomes convincing when valuable salvage and readable danger belong to the same place.
The strongest objection: this could become an efficient resource factory with eerie decoration, or a collection of unrelated punishments. Passing def reads would establish neither the curse nor the worthwhile expedition. The root is a proposed promise, not a report that all of it works.

These are eight drafts for a question bank. **Ask 01 and 02 first**, then serve at most three at a time after independent review. A phrase, a different scene, or “this doesn't matter to me” can change the proposal. Silence approves nothing. All pictures below are written thought experiments, not captures, visual mocks or staged gameplay. Any later choice about actual appearance or motion needs something to look at.

01. ASK FIRST — The promise behind the curse

Picture: The crew brings home a working projector and an intact machine. One scavenger carries a lasting mark. They understand the risks better than on arrival, and already have a reason to go back.

Question: What should make you think “they learned why it is cursed” in that return-home moment, and which part of my root gets that wrong?

Why it matters: F1/F3. “Cursed” could rest on an enduring personal cost, unsettling discoveries, or the way practical gains tempt another visit. The chosen sentence does not rank those consequences.

My guess, not your answer: The temptation matters because the costs are real and remembered. I would lead with a successful expedition that changes somebody.

What changes in the tree: If the personal cost leads, the return-home scene must show the lasting mark alongside the prize. If discovery leads, the scene must connect what was found to the battlefield without revealing campaign secrets. If temptation leads, it must show a concrete next expedition worth its price. These are emphasis and acceptance choices; all built systems retain coverage. A correction rewrites the root before dependent claims are bound.

Why it matters: F1/F2/F3. I found no recorded choice of this mod's flagship under the current showcase contract.

03. The invisible hunter while you are watching somewhere else

Picture: A lone night hauler passes through glower. A moving patch of ticking falls silent; when there is film, prints cross it with no animal above them. The camera is at the workshop. A second colonist is close enough to make the hauler company.

Question: Which of those signs should reach you in time to change the hauler's orders, and which may be a discovery you make afterwards?

Why it matters: F2. Prints, silence, shimmer and strike reveal are already built or specified. Their existence alone does not settle whether an off-camera ambush was fairly readable.

My guess, not your answer: On camera, movement and silence should give an attentive player a chance; ordinary off-camera travel should remain manageable through company rather than a new warning for every hunter.

What changes in the tree: If local observation is enough, judge an actual approach at play zoom and with sound, plus the existing company protection. If a remote warning is essential, add a narrowly triggered warning and a control with no approaching hunter. If signs may explain the aftermath, require a trace that remains interpretable after the attack; do not silently claim advance warning. Group safety remains an existing obligation in every branch.

04. What the chatrak's warning buys

Picture: A scaria chatrak near the work area lifts its plates, stops feeding and begins circling. Workers are dispersed; one animal is tethered nearby. The stages and eventual rage are already part of the build.

Question: By the circling stage, what should a careful player still have time to get out of its way?

Why it matters: F2. Setting a stage directly proves its state, but says nothing about the useful time between noticing it and the snap.

My guess, not your answer: Nearby people and a sensibly managed animal should have a fair retreat; a whole settlement should not be rebuilt during that last warning.

What changes in the tree: A local retreat requires an observed warning-to-danger interval with ordinary movement and a blocked-route reject case. A whole-worksite evacuation needs a dispersed-party scene and enough earlier notice. If circling is already the point of no safe response, bind the earlier plate change as the actionable warning and judge circling as imminent danger. None of these branches reopens scaria onset or the settled clean-animal distinction.

05. Repair when the safer part is late

Picture: A named deserter has reached the limbs stage. Etchant is available, but the haulers are busy. The current cradle waits six in-game hours, then starts a slower and riskier repair without it. Its memories and eventual usefulness are already part of the promise.

Question: What should tell the cradle to take that extra risk when the safer part is merely late: your order, a standing repair policy, or elapsed time?

Why it matters: F1/F3. Repair without etchant is already allowed. The unresolved choice is who commits a particular machine to that risk when a routine hauling delay looks the same as a shortage.

My guess, not your answer: A standing repair policy, visible on the cradle, with an explicit choice for this machine. I would default to waiting when etchant exists and is reachable.

What changes in the tree: A direct order needs a waiting state and an explicit proceed-without-etchant action. A standing policy needs saved per-cradle behaviour and a test with delayed hauling. Elapsed time needs the deadline and extra risk stated before it expires, plus a clear account of the failure if it occurs. Every branch preserves the etchant-free repair route, the named failure wreck, the accepted histories and the strong worker.

06. Taking the shelter home

Picture: The only working ring shelters the party's supplies and an injured scavenger. Its condition has been evaluated. A colonist finishes uninstalling it, and the clean circle disappears while the Settling continues. Losing the dome when the ring is taken is already the intended trade.

Question: Before that last action completes, what should make the exposed people and supplies clear to you: the order's explanation, a warning when work begins, or the changing scene itself?

Why it matters: F1/F2. A salvage job can be mechanically correct while hiding the consequence from the person who ordered it.

My guess, not your answer: The order should state the loss of shelter clearly; an extra interruption earns its place only when somebody is actually relying on that dome.

What changes in the tree: An order explanation needs a truthful command description and a consequence check. A conditional warning needs a defined dependency test and a control where another screen still protects the occupants. Scene-only communication needs a witnessed transition that makes the exposed area clear without reading an alert. Working-ring salvage stays available in every case.

07. The beautiful phase that burns

Picture: Before the phase reader is unlocked, a tap shows an unknown colour and a phase icon. The colony has ordered repeated draws. During the walk to the tap the pool changes to bloom; drawing it produces valuable liquor and burns the worker's hand.

Question: What should let you distinguish a deliberate dangerous harvest from an ordinary job that unexpectedly burns its worker?

Why it matters: F1/F2. The bloom's injury, journal and later skip control are already specified. The missing acceptance choice is what the order commits the player to before that safety knowledge exists.

My guess, not your answer: The standing order should plainly include the dangerous phase, with a readable last chance to stop it. The phase reader can still provide the better automatic control later.

What changes in the tree: If the order carries the warning, require an explicit risk description and a phase-change-during-walk case. If the changing phase must communicate the risk, judge a labelled exploration of that transition before prescribing a cue. If the first injury teaches the consequence, bind the lasting journal and cause-of-injury explanation, and do not claim the first draw was warned safe. Existing bloom harvesting and the researched skip control remain in all branches.

08. A settlement large enough to miss things

Picture: Forty colonists have an established workday here. The wind stills while one cradle waits for a part, a tap is drawing, and crews are sweeping dust and salvaging. The mark, risks and useful jobs continue; the player has other colony problems too.

Question: Which change should interrupt that workday, and which should the colony handle through standing orders while you watch elsewhere?

Why it matters: F1/F3. “Atmosphere” can become constant alert dismissal, and “danger” can become unnoticed harm. The source sets mechanics and some messages, but does not establish an attention budget for mature play.

My guess, not your answer: A newly unsafe work area deserves attention; routine known production and waiting for a part should remain ordinary work, with details available on inspection.

What changes in the tree: If new exposure is the interruption, add a shared-condition scene with protected and exposed crews. If each major event demands attention, judge the actual combined message sequence and its deduplication. If standing orders should cover known hazards, specify which order is relied on and show it working while another task occupies the player. This selects communication and routine-work bars, not permission to suppress any built capability.
