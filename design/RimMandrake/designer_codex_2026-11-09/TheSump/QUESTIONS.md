PROPOSED ROOT

TheSump makes settling a cold tar basin a struggle over ordinary actions: moving, working, staying clean and getting back what the black has kept. A colony learns to read its mice and plants, chooses routes through sticky ground, and turns the same troublesome tar into useful materials, beautiful gaslight and a pantry that preserves at the price of solvent. Digging buys a chance at deep-time finds while risking a preserved trap or something much larger below. Channels and gates let the colony put tar to work, but a belch or a careless burn can change the ground it thought it understood. An ordinarily woken tar beast is a catastrophe to evacuate ahead of; deliberately pouring solvent wakes a different danger that hunts the map. The tools make a messy settlement possible without making the Sump surrender.

The existing owner experience is the foundation, quoted from the TheSump row in `design/RimMandrake/biome_experiences_2026-10-20.md`, under “The experience, in the owner's words” (typed, 2026-10-20):

> Sticky, stinking, slow to move or burn or surrender. The sump hides all and makes you fight it for every action.

That existing sentence is his; the proposed paragraph above is mine. Its approval has not happened.

My claim: ordinary resistance, and learning to put the same tar to use, join the mod's many built parts.
The strongest objection: the beautiful root can promise truthful clues and a working daily economy that the current isolated tests do not establish. Several plants have bodies without their special behaviour; some essential tar tracking still lives in the campaign patch. This is a proposed player promise, not a claim that the mod passes.

These eight drafts are a bank. Start with **01 and 02**, then choose at most three per real turn as the answers make the others useful. A phrase, a different scene, or “this doesn't matter to me” is enough. Silence approves nothing. No question asks whether an existing capability should survive.

Every picture below is a **written thought experiment**, not a game capture, visual mock, staged test or accepted scene. We must show a labelled visual or exploration before binding an answer about actual appearance.

01. The ordinary moment — ASK FIRST

Picture to react to: A worker carries lunch across black ground, brings tar indoors, and gets the useful find home only after another awkward journey. That evening the same colony sits beside a lamp made from tar and acid.

Question: Which ordinary moment makes that your Sump, and where does the proposed root give you the wrong picture?

Why it matters: F1/F2. A catalogue of hazards can miss your sentence's force: even an ordinary errand must feel like dealing with this place.

My guess, not your answer: The everyday struggle comes first, with clever use of the same tar making life possible. I recommend that as the root's centre.

What changes in the tree: If everyday resistance leads, root acceptance follows a complete working day. If reading hidden danger leads, lead with a careful expedition and its decisions. If making use of tar leads, lead with production and preservation. All existing capabilities retain coverage; a correction rewrites dependent claims. No answer approves a root.

02. The first demonstration — ASK FIRST

Picture to react to: A: a first expedition reads the mice, works a shaft, and hears the click. B: forty colonists keep food, routes and lamps working through a normal dirty day. C: a defended station operates its tar channels and firebreaks, then evacuates ahead of the ordinary station-eating beast. These are three scene drafts, not captures or accepted demonstrations.

Question: Which scene goes first when you decide whether we understood the Sump, and what failure there would stop you?

Why it matters: F1–F3. No flagship pick was found. A spectacular fire could hide an empty daily life; a tidy little test could hide a colony that cannot cope.

My guess, not your answer: B first: your experience sentence is about every action. A follows to test the hidden dangers, then C for consequences at a larger scale.

What changes in the tree: A selects expedition as showcase 1; B selects ordinary colony life; C selects station emergency. The actual owner pick must later be recorded separately from root approval. Other independent capabilities keep their scenes; later showcases must add different functionality.

03. A settlement that knows what it is doing

Picture to react to: The colony now has forty people, glass routes, stocked solvent, a pantry and dependable lamps. The player has learned the place and supplied the coping tools. Tar still comes home from outside work.

Question: What still takes your attention on an ordinary day here, and what should the colony now manage for itself?

Why it matters: F2/F3. Your dirty-colony rule and generous local solvent are settled. The unresolved bar is whether mature play means recurring decisions or repeatedly issuing the same cleaning and retrieval orders.

My guess, not your answer: I still choose routes, work sites and where to spend effort; stocked routine cleaning and food handling run through ordinary colony work. The colony stays dirty without constant repeated clicks.

What changes in the tree: If routine care should run itself, require repeatable ordinary jobs and stock/solvent controls at colony scale. If hands-on extraction is part of play, require useful batch orders and an explicit amount of attention. If only special emergencies need attention, tighten the routine-day bar. This clarifies functioning tools, without reopening either walkway or preservation system.

04. Between the click and the blast

Picture to react to: A shaft arms a trap while the good miner is elsewhere. A novice also has Mining enabled. The current code shows a countdown only when inspected and can send that novice to disarm automatically; failing its skill gate detonates it. The high-skill disarm rule itself is already settled.

Question: What should you be able to do between noticing the click and the explosion, and what would make losing that novice feel deserved?

Why it matters: F1/F3. A real chance to react is different from a fuse that runs unnoticed while an automatic job makes the fatal choice.

My guess, not your answer: I can stop the nearby work, get people clear, and send a suitable miner if one is close enough. A novice touching it should follow an informed choice, rather than a surprise automatic reassignment.

What changes in the tree: If the choice is local retreat, assert time and a readable cue for workers already there. If selecting a specialist is essential, require a deliberate or correctly gated assignment and demonstrate a nearby specialist reaching it. If automatic response is intended, require that its eligibility and lethal risk are clear before normal work assigns it. All branches preserve skilled disarm and failure detonation.

05. What a successful evacuation saves

Picture to react to: The ordinary beast wakes after disturbance. People can walk away faster than it moves, but the dense station contains stores, beds and production. This is the station-eater, separate from the deliberately poured-solvent manhunter weapon. A day-ahead warning is already in its specification.

Question: When you call that evacuation a success, what has the colony carried away, and what has the tar taken back?

Why it matters: F2/F3. Outwalking a slow pawn proves little if recovering anything useful is impossible, or if a giant's appetite can be satisfied by a few throwaway fixtures.

My guess, not your answer: People and a chosen portion of valuable stores can escape; saving every building is not the bargain. I recommend a costly retreat with real choices, rather than a guaranteed loss or a cheap decoy solution.

What changes in the tree: People-only success sets a human survival scene and bounded warning. A meaningful salvage success adds hauling time, access and a limited useful recovery target. Relocation of most operations requires a longer preparation/recovery case. If a cheap decoy is an intended tactic, make that limit explicit; otherwise test it as a counterexample to station-eating. No answer reopens the beast's existence or the separate solvent weapon.

06. Yesterday's mouse-line after a belch

Picture to react to: A belch has changed a route. Old tracks remain where workers remember walking; the new ground and plants have not yet settled. Later the mice and margins redraw. Honest mouse tells and slow living-map response are already required; their safe relationship during that interval is not spelled out.

Question: What should warn a careful returning worker's player that yesterday's route no longer tells the truth?

Why it matters: F1. A living map can pass a plant-spawn test while its old signs actively mislead the player during the dangerous hours.

My guess, not your answer: The fresh change in the ground is legible and old tracks cease to look like current evidence; new mouse behaviour becomes trustworthy as it appears. I would start with the ground and the animals themselves, consistent with the existing rule against a mouse-warning overlay.

What changes in the tree: If the new surface is sufficient, bind a read of an actual rewritten route with stale tracks still present. If old tracks must disappear or visibly age, require their transition and a control with an unchanged route. If the worker must wait for fresh mouse traffic, state that rule and demonstrate a practical way to distinguish old from new evidence. Any appearance choice needs a labelled mock or game exploration before a visual leaf is fixed.

07. What makes another day's digging worth it

Picture to react to: The colony has spent a day working the tar and has had to deal with a trap. Most current lottery finds are ordinary salvage; the preserved draw-joint is a real exception that teaches useful machinery. The ruled archive of older remains and machines has much more character than those placeholders.

Question: What single find would change tomorrow's plans enough to make that day's risk worthwhile?

Why it matters: F2. A weighted table can work perfectly while the archive is merely a slow source of sellable junk. The prize must affect play, not just carry an ancient-sounding label.

My guess, not your answer: A useful preserved mechanism that changes what I can build or safely attempt, with remains and lesser salvage making the dig feel like deep time. The draw-joint is a good concrete starting example.

What changes in the tree: A new practical tool requires a complete find-to-use scene, extending the existing learning model only as needed. Valuable material requires a meaningful production/trade decision. Historical discovery requires a recognisable reveal and a reason to care. A dangerous ancient machine adds a separately bounded wake/recovery scene. Whole prohibited ancient bodies and ordinary live-preserved fauna stay ruled out under every answer.

08. Two ways to put food under tar

Picture to react to: A haul of food comes home. The shelf vault preserves the original and charges solvent to retrieve it; with Cold Storage installed, the vat instead makes sealed food that can age into pitch-aged food. Both systems are built, and bodies and hides also give the vault a wider job.

Question: What would make you put one haul in the vault and the next into sealed jars?

Why it matters: F2/F3. These two built preservation systems arrived through different work. Their combined daily play needs a clear reason for choosing, without inventing a second solvent tax or making one a hidden trap.

My guess, not your answer: The vault is flexible storage for food and other perishables I want back unchanged; the jars are deliberate food preparation, portable supplies and aging for a better ingredient. I recommend proving that distinction with ordinary cook and haul work.

What changes in the tree: If immediate flexible storage versus prepared/aged food is the choice, require both useful routes and an ordinary meal/hauling scene. If one is the emergency option, bind its access/time tradeoff. If there is a different distinction, derive only that contract. If the distinction does not matter, treat it as advisory while still covering both built systems; neither is offered for removal.
