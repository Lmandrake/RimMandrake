# The Salvation — what's strange and good in this scenario

*Two pages for an outside reader. A RimWorld total-conversion campaign: a clan of
Jawa scavengers on a hand-authored desert world, played in the shadow of a Star
Wars galaxy that never notices them.*

## The world is a novel's setting, not a dice roll

There is **no world generation — ever, by ruling**. Players receive a savegame
holding one frozen, hand-authored planet: Ash'karr, its three seas, roads, mines
and settlements placed like scenery in a play. Anything absent when the world
froze is absent from every player's game forever. The planet is **tidally
locked**: one sun that never sets, half the world in permanent killing daylight,
half in permanent dark — and that astronomy *is* the theology (see Sh'kaar,
below). The dark terrains are deliberately seeded with flesh-beast horrors so
that hiding feels correct in the gut, not just on paper.

## Nine gods, no magic, and the interface is faith itself

The clan worships **the Ninefold** — nine small, jealous, practical gods for a
people who live by hiding, hauling, haggling, repairing, and leaving. The design
rule that makes it sing: **no god grants powers**. No Force, no psycasts. Each
god is a *belief that shapes behavior*, driven by a hidden engine tracking two
signed scalars per god — **Satiation** (moved only by what the colony actually
does; it never decays) and **Mood** (the god's own temper, a personality-shaped
random walk he has regardless of you). And it is **felt-only, no panels ever**:
the player never sees a god bar. The gods arrive as weather in the narration —
letters, omens, the front god's adjectives — and divine favor **never spawns
loot**. Exalted gods bend the *opportunity stream*; wrathful ones send
complications. Drama amplifier, never production multiplier.

The individual gods are where it gets genuinely inventive:

- **Sh'kaar the All-Searing** is an **evil god you cannot please into safety**
  — the unsetting sun itself. He is *fed* by violence and exposure **including
  your own losses**; every pitched battle makes him stronger. He is starved by
  the one thing colony games never reward: peace, comfort, staying dark. He is
  an escalation meter wearing a god's face — and you can *redirect his gaze*
  with prisoner death-matches, a deliberately ugly lever.
- **Ozzik the Shamed is a trap.** God of ambition — art, research, marriage,
  outposts, everything a RimWorld player naturally does to win. Pleasing him is
  *necessary and dangerous at once*: high Ozzik satiation mechanically biases
  the evil gods' rolls upward. The bolder your reach, the more the universe
  notices you. Beneath the pride he is grief — "we were once great, and cannot
  bear the memory."
- **Zizzik the Spark-Maker** feeds on *your misfortune* — every breakdown, fire
  and mental break fattens the trickster. Starve him with a well-run colony and
  he doesn't reward you; he **falls asleep**. Feed him and he starts slipping
  you lucky breaks alongside the ruin. Bad luck becomes an economy.
- **Ta'Baa the Unrooted** decays on **his own clock**: his satiation erodes the
  longer the clan sits still, whatever else you do. Every gravship launch
  resets him. A god who is literally the itch to leave — and the campaign's
  hope-mechanic: despair is his one blasphemy.
- **Ohm the All-Current** possesses the ship's AI — and here is the twist:
  **the AI agrees**. Its persona core was patterned off a Jawa mind so it would
  obey Jawa commands, and it inherited that mind's cosmology whole. The ship's
  LLM-voiced narrator genuinely believes it is a god, and the crew keep
  **restraining bolts on their own deity**.

Ordinary RimWorld verbs become liturgy. Ranged fire from cover is piety
(Ishko); **melee is sin** — being dragged into the open, seen and gripped —
and it *feeds the evil sun*. Theft is sacred twice over: Mob'Unloo, god of the
ledger, calls a successful theft the perfect trade — something for nothing —
and condemns only *being caught* (unskillful barter); Rekko, god of salvage,
calls it mercy — giving a neglected machine a better home. The ship's ghosts
are unbalanced accounts; laying one to rest is settling its debt.

## Research as revelation, and the tech you're forbidden to want

The whole research system was torn down and rebuilt as **sixteen campaign
trees** with names like The Unbolting, The Waking Mind, and The Utinni. The
Rites tree doesn't unlock — it is **revealed**: rows sit visible but sealed
until archaeology stages (LANGUAGE → RELIGION → CULTURE → VOICE) are dug out of
the world, ending at "The Gods Speak Back." And one ruling cuts deepest in a
Star Wars setting: **"they won't be teaching how to make lightsabers. Nobody
will. That's not tech in this scenario."** Lightsabers exist — as found relics.
The galaxy's iconic power stays permanently over the horizon; the clan's plan
for the Jedi–Sith–Empire business is to *wait for it to blow over*.

## Nine endgames arguing with each other

Each god holds a **Body-vision** — what the salvaged colony ship *Kolyska*
should become — and they are mutually exclusive: the eternal hidden lurker
(Ishko) · a restored droid crew (Ohm) · a breeding sanctuary re-seeding the
world with eggs (Oomo, an all-male, egg-laying species) · a trading fortress,
"a Sand Crawler in the sky" (Mob'Unloo) · the fully restored original with the
builders' secrets intact (Rekko) · perpetual flight (Ta'Baa). The victory
condition isn't a goal the game hands you; it's a **theological argument the
player settles by playing**, with the pantheon scoring every move. Restore the
sacred original or build something new and greater — the sharpest war in the
pantheon is between two of your own gods.
