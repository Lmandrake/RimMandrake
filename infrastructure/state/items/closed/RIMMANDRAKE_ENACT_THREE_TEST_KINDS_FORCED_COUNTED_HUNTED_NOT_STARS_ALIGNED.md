**Question:** Mon Nov 23, 11:40 AM PST. Pushback 1 of 4, on kind B (a stochastic test: a reliable capability whose test outcome is unreliable because of many environment variables). The alternative is to demand an explanation: each failed run is read and classed as environment, test apparatus or mod, and a failure nobody can explain is treated as a possible mod bug and moves to kind C (the hunt for an intermittent bug). (Hard to read? Type STE, Binding or Both for a rewrite.) How should a kind B test be judged as passed?
**His words:** For Tests of Type A (intended for capabilities that should be reliable and reliably testable) you would not use "4 of 5" form of success. That would be inappropriate. If there are environmental variables that frequently confound an otherwise reliable capability (such as requiring certain pawns present in specific onfigurations), it becomes test type B where traced success & failures is important evidence but ANY demonstration of success may be as good as we're going to get. Then tuning the X out of Y numbers becomes art and resource balancing. Please push back if this doesn't seem appropriate or make sense or address your concern.

**Question:** Pushback 2 of 4, on kind C (an intermittent test: the test is deterministic, but it hunts a bug that shows only sometimes, so it looks for evidence of failure among apparent successes). The danger is that a hunt has no natural end: today's rim-rescue bug showed once in 18 free runs, and proving 'gone' at that rate by clean runs alone needs about 54 runs. I propose that a C hunt ends in one of two ways only: the trigger is found and forced (then it becomes a kind A test, as happened today), or a fixed hunt budget runs out and the bug is left with a tripwire, which is a cheap check that rides inside every other run and goes red with a trace if the bug shows again. The open question is your pass bar. When a mod still has a known intermittent bug with no cause found, may that mod pass its Design Version?
**His words:** Intermittent bugs will be a transient hunt mode, usually by a test pilot, but possibly temporarily inserted into passing pipeline tests while they are still being hunted during the development (after having been seen once or twice). They are never intended to be left on for the final testing suite. As you say, once their source is detected, they may be converted into specific forced configurations (hopefully even deterministic at that point) to ensure it does not reoccur, but after the fix that requires proof of likely re-emergence (you don't want your basic acceptance suite turning into a littany of everything that was once wrong, it's too expensive for live game testing). Offline tests may be more receptive to holding a large number of "once wrong" recursion testing. If the bug is never or exceptionally rarely reproducable and cannot be transformed into a forced test, it is dropped from any mod acceptance testing to preserve efficiency. This should become a broad principle in our game: the Principle of Impactful Evidence: due to fixed resources, we must allocate testing only to what is likely to provide future value, never comprehensive coverage for its own sake, and that equation will change over time given evolving knowledge of an evolving system. **Question:** Pushback 4 of 4, on 'force the situation ALWAYS'. I agree for the environment: place the raider, order the job, pin the carrier. Two risks. First, forcing can stage a situation the real game never produces, and we then fix a bug no player meets. Second, sometimes the choice is our own mod's code (for example our pathing patch decides whether a raider enters a pit); forcing past that choice skips the code under test. So I propose the rule 'force the environment, never the mod's own decision; the mod's decision gets its own forced-input test'. That leaves the natural, unforced run (a real raid left to do what it does). What place should unforced natural runs keep?
**His words:** Agreed, forcing is dangerous, not simply preferred. It tests the basic, elemental capability but does not explore important configurations. Here is another Principle, this time for Debugging/testing skills in rimworld: the minimum force required for testing is optimal. Example: We are testing a raider walking over a large covered pit, but they rarely choose to walk over it in a real game. From least desirable/most elemental to most desirable/most complete: teleport raider onto middle of covered pit; pre-set raider walk path over the pit; place pit in narrow hallway without alternative route and place desired object/combatant as bait to draw them over it; place an array of covered pits entirely surrounding a natural base and allow naturally spawned raiders to find their own path across in their own time. [We will almost never be able to afford that final, natural test type due to the time and token cost of each test plus a potentially low yield due to environmental factors and other mod interactions] [Agreed that the player walkthrough can cover the most complex situations, but even still some will simply have to wait for real gameplay to discover, and that's ok. that is the "testing horizon" we can afford.]
